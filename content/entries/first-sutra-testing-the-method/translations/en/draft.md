# The First Sūtra: Testing the Method

This is an entry about the first practical test of the method described in the [methodology note](https://unfolding.day/entries/patanjalayogashastra-methodology).

The text of the first sūtra and Vyāsa’s commentary, with Russian and English translations, notes and grammatical analysis, is published separately: [Pātañjalayogaśāstra 1.1 — Research Translation with Vyāsa’s Commentary](https://unfolding.day/entries/patanjalayogashastra-1-1).

This entry looks at the research process: which stages of the original method could be carried out, which tools were used, what could be verified, where the limits turned out to be, and which questions remained open.

## 1. The aim of the experiment

YS 1.1 was chosen for the first test: the sūtra *atha yogānuśāsanam* and the nine sentences of Vyāsa’s accompanying commentary (*Vyāsabhāṣya*).

The small size of the text made it possible to study every word form in detail, trace the grammatical relations, check meanings against dictionaries and compare our own translation with an academic one.

The aim was not only to produce a translation but also to test the research process itself: whether the proposed method can be applied consistently, the grounds of translation decisions documented, and questions for which the available evidence is insufficient kept open.

## 2. Method: plan and practice

The original methodology envisaged the following sequence:

*Sanskrit text → segmentation and morphology → dictionaries → syntactic analysis → earlier Yoga contexts where needed → literal Russian and English translation → comparison with academic translations → investigation of divergences.*

Results of the first test:

| Planned stage | Actual result |
| --- | --- |
| Source Sanskrit text | GRETIL, based on the 1904 Āgāśe edition |
| Devanāgarī and IAST | All ten passages fixed |
| Segmentation and morphology | 56 distinct word forms (64 occurrences) checked with the Sanskrit Heritage Reader |
| Dictionary check | Monier-Williams and Apte used |
| Additional dictionaries and sources for disputed points | Not consulted; unresolved questions recorded |
| Syntactic analysis | Done; alternative readings documented |
| Pāṇini rules | Used in addition; the eight rules cited were checked against Böhtlingk’s edition |
| Earlier Yoga texts | Not consulted; originally envisaged as an optional source |
| Russian and English translations | Both editions prepared and aligned |
| Woods (1914) | Control comparison with analysis of divergences |
| Larson (2018) | Not used because the full text was unavailable |
| Recheck of divergences | Two divergences rechecked against the dictionaries; others referred to the author or left open |

Thus the main stages of the research cycle were carried out. Of the two academic control translations originally envisaged, one was used, Woods (1914): the full text of Larson (2018) could not be obtained during the work. Additional dictionaries and sources were not consulted to resolve disputed points; the corresponding questions are kept open.

Earlier Yoga texts were not a mandatory stage. They were to be used where additional historical context was needed to establish a meaning. This stage was not carried out in the first study.

## 3. Tools and reproducibility

**Source text.** The working copy of the sūtra and the commentary was obtained from [GRETIL](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_pataJjali-yogasUtra-with-bhASya.htm). Philipp A. Maas’s electronic transcription is based on the 1904 edition by Kāśinātha Śāstrī Āgāśe.

The YS 1.1 passage was stored with checksums so that later checks would rest on one and the same textual basis.

**Morphological analysis.** The [Sanskrit Heritage Reader](https://sanskrit.inria.fr/DICO/reader.html) was used for segmentation and analysis of 56 distinct word forms (64 occurrences). Checks were made both on isolated word forms and in the context of sentences.

The automatic analysis brought out grammatical alternatives but did not always determine a single admissible reading. Such cases were examined separately and kept in a list of unresolved questions.

**Dictionaries.** Meanings were checked against Monier-Williams (1899) and Apte (1957–1959) in the [Cologne Digital Sanskrit Dictionaries](https://www.sanskrit-lexicon.uni-koeln.de/).

The dictionary entries used were extracted unchanged and stored with checksums. The analysis took the origin of senses into account: if a dictionary referred to the Yoga tradition itself or to later texts, such a sense was not treated as independent confirmation of an interpretation.

**Pāṇini’s grammar.** The *Aṣṭādhyāyī* was consulted to check particular word-formation constructions and compounds. In preparing the publication, all eight rules used were checked again against [Otto Böhtlingk, *Pâṇini’s Grammatik* (1887)](https://archive.org/details/grammatikhrsgb00paniuoft).

**Translation and research documentation.** The Russian translation was made directly from the Sanskrit and fixed before Woods’s control translation was consulted. The English edition was prepared on the same Sanskrit basis and aligned with the Russian one.

In both published versions the Devanāgarī and IAST are identical byte for byte in all ten passages. Translation decisions and permitted differences between the languages are recorded in the research materials.

A substantial part of the technical work was done with an AI agent: extracting and organizing source data, preparing grammatical tables, comparing dictionary evidence, identifying divergences and formatting the results.

The agent was not, however, the final arbiter of meaning. Disputed readings were examined separately, and the decisions included in the published translation were checked by the author.

The stored source passages, checksums, grammatical tables, dictionary materials and the preliminary version of the translation make it possible to reconstruct the grounds of the decisions taken.

Full bibliographic details are given in the Sources and Abbreviations section of the translation.

## 4. Findings from the Independent Translation Check

After the preliminary translation had been fixed, a control comparison was made with [James Haughton Woods, *The Yoga-System of Patañjali* (1914)](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.40668).

Woods’s translation served as a control comparison, not as a source for the initial choice of meanings.

Divergences were classified as stylistic, lexical or interpretive. Agreement was not treated as proof of correctness, and a divergence by itself did not mean an error. In each case it was necessary to return to the Sanskrit text, the grammar and the dictionary evidence.

**The translation of *adhikṛta*.**

The second passage of the commentary contains the word *adhikṛta*, which Woods renders as *commenced*.

The check against Apte and Monier-Williams supports the sense “made the subject”: Apte gives a corresponding example of a passive form with an inanimate subject, and Monier-Williams records a close sense for *adhikṛtya*.

The sense “begin” was not found in the examined entries of this word family. Our translation therefore keeps the wording “taken as the subject of the exposition”.

Woods’s reading may be an interpretive paraphrase of the context of the opening of a work, but it does not follow directly from the dictionary senses that were checked.

**The difference between *kṣipta* and *vikṣipta*.**

Woods translates these states as *restless* and *distracted*.

Morphological analysis shows that the forms differ by the prefix *vi-*. The dictionaries record partly different shades of meaning, but part of the evidence belongs to the later tradition, and Apte’s entry quotes the very sentence under study. Woods’s distinction between *restless* and *distracted* is not independently supported by the dictionary check applied, by morphology or by the nearest context. The precise substantive distinction between the two terms therefore remains unestablished.

Both terms have therefore been retained in Sanskrit transliteration, and the question of their relation remains open.

**The meaning of *sārvabhauma*.**

In the fourth passage of the commentary, Woods renders *sārvabhauma* as belonging to “all the stages”, thereby connecting it with the list of *cittabhūmi* in the next sentence.

This connection is possible and is supported by the context, but its precise meaning has not been established by grammatical and dictionary analysis. The research translation does not assert it as a final reading.

**The phrase *nirodham abhimukhaṃ karoti*.**

In the seventh passage, Woods offers an interpretation that sets *nirodha* as a goal, adding content about *vṛtti* that is absent from the sentence itself.

Our grammatical analysis left several possible constructions of this phrase. The control comparison did not provide sufficient grounds for choosing one of them.

The expression is therefore kept in its Sanskrit form.

**Result of the control comparison.**

The rechecks of two divergences, *adhikṛta* and *kṣipta*/*vikṣipta*, gave no grounds to change the translation in substance. Other divergences required the author’s decisions or remained open. The final edition did, however, refine a number of formulations by the author’s decisions, including in the first, second and eighth passages of the commentary.

The comparison also brought out an important feature of academic translations: they may contain interpretations based on the wider context of the work or on the later commentarial tradition. Such decisions must be distinguished from meanings established directly by the passage under study.

The additional check of Pāṇini’s rules confirmed all eight rules used, including the peculiarity of sūtra 5.4.50: the condition *abhūtatadbhāve* is absent from the text of the sūtra itself but is introduced in the commentarial tradition, as Böhtlingk points out.

In preparing the publication, an inaccuracy in a dictionary reference was also corrected: the abbreviation “Bhag.” was replaced by “Bg.” in accordance with Apte’s designation of the source.

## 5. Limits of the research

The research made it possible to establish grammatical constructions, identify ranges of meaning and check translation decisions, but not all questions received an unambiguous answer.

The dictionary check was limited to Monier-Williams and Apte. Additional dictionaries and other evidence were not consulted even for some disputed points, so the corresponding questions remained open. The absence of a sense in these two dictionaries does not mean that it is absent from the whole Sanskrit corpus.

Some senses of technical terms in the dictionaries are based on later Yoga texts. They may therefore reflect a later interpretation of the concept rather than its meaning in the passage under study.

The control comparison with Woods was made on machine-recognized text. The English wording used in the comparison was not additionally checked against scans of the printed edition.

The second control translation originally planned was Gerald James Larson, *Classical Yoga Philosophy and the Legacy of Sāṃkhya* (2018). The full text of this edition, however, proved unavailable, so YS 1.1 was compared with Woods only.

The sources of the original check of Pāṇini’s rules were not fully recorded. In preparing the publication, the rules were checked again against Böhtlingk’s edition.

Some abbreviations in dictionary entries also remained not fully resolved: *Kap.* in Monier-Williams points to Kapila without a specific work, and *Sch.* denotes a commentator without naming them.

### Open questions

1. Is *asaṃprajñāta samādhi* also *yoga*? In the text under study, *yoga* is the name explicitly given to the *ekāgra* case in the seventh passage.
2. How are *niruddha* (fifth passage), *nirodham* (seventh) and the *nirodha* of all *vṛtti* (ninth) related?
3. What is the precise syntax of the expression *nirodham abhimukhaṃ karoti*?
4. How is *sārvabhauma* (fourth passage) related to the list of *cittabhūmi* (fifth)?

In addition, the precise meaning of *bhūmi* and the difference in substance between *kṣipta* and *vikṣipta* remain unresolved.

One result of the research was the decision not to replace such uncertainties with familiar translation equivalents. Where the available evidence does not allow one meaning to be chosen on good grounds, the Sanskrit term is kept in transliteration together with an explanation of the possible readings.

The principles behind this decision will be discussed in detail in a separate note, “Making Room for the Unknown”.

## 6. What will change in the work on YS 1.2

The first test confirmed that the main method is applicable and, at the same time, showed the need to separate automatic operations, interpretive decisions and the author’s review more precisely.

The next experiment will be a more autonomous study of YS 1.2 using an AI agent.

The agent is to go through the sequence of operations on its own: fix the source Sanskrit text, carry out segmentation and morphological analysis, check dictionary senses, investigate syntactic constructions, prepare separate preliminary Russian and English translations and fix them before consulting Woods’s control translation. It must then classify the divergences, recheck the significant cases and document the decisions taken or deferred.

For the research to be reproducible, it is necessary to keep not only the result but also the prompts used, the source data, the tools’ outputs, the divergences found and the grounds for later corrections. The specific editions and places against which Pāṇini’s rules are checked should be recorded, and when working with Woods, significant quotations should be checked against a scan of the printed edition rather than relying only on recognized text.

Cases in which the automatic check does not give an unambiguous result must be recorded separately. The agent must not resolve them by arbitrarily choosing the most familiar interpretation.

After the autonomous pass is complete, the results will be reviewed by a person. This will make it possible to compare the agent’s work with the process of studying the first sūtra and to determine which operations can be reliably automated and which require direct research involvement.

### Refining the Method for Subsequent Research

Based on the first test, the decision was made to divide the work on the *Pātañjalayogaśāstra* into two independent research stages.

**Stage one: a complete translation of the work.**

The main process is kept: Sanskrit text, morphological analysis, dictionaries, syntactic analysis, independent Russian and English translations, control against Woods and investigation of divergences. For significant disputed senses, a separate check against additional dictionaries is permitted, with its sources and result recorded.

Larson is excluded from the mandatory control comparison because the full text is unavailable.

Recourse to earlier Yoga works is likewise moved out of the main translation process. Comparisons with other sūtras and passages of the *Pātañjalayogaśāstra* itself are kept as a separate stage after the initial analysis of each passage. Passages that come later in the order of exposition must not silently replace the evidence of the sentence under study: their influence on the interpretation should be marked explicitly.

**Stage two: historical and comparative research.**

After the complete translation is prepared, a separate study of earlier relevant Sanskrit texts is envisaged. Translating them independently will later make it possible to compare terminology, philosophical concepts and contexts with the *Pātañjalayogaśāstra*.

Such a comparison may reveal continuity, shifts in meaning and possible historical connections. Conclusions about the influence of one text on another will require their own argument.

Separating these stages will make it possible first to study the work on its own textual basis and then to consider it in a wider historical context.

## Outcome

The test on the first sūtra showed that the proposed method is workable in practice.

The main research cycle could be completed: from fixing the Sanskrit text and the grammatical analysis to an independent translation, dictionary checks and a control comparison with Woods. Of the two control editions originally planned, one, Larson, could not be used because the full text was not accessible. Additional sources for some disputed points were also not consulted, and the unresolved questions remain documented.

The results made it possible to justify the translation decisions, find the limits of the available evidence and keep the unresolved questions without premature interpretation.

The next step is to test, on YS 1.2, how far an AI agent can reproduce this process on its own.
