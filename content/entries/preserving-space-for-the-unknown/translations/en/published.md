# Preserving Space for the Unknown

Having already decided to translate the *Pātañjalayogaśāstra* and begun working on the first sūtra and its commentary, I listened several times to two translations of the *Yoga Sūtras*. They were good, evocative translations that were easy to follow: they inspired practice and were well suited to a first encounter with the text. But as I compared them with the original, I increasingly felt how much more deeply I could explore the meaning of the text by trying to understand it for myself.

At the beginning of my work on Patañjali's text, following the chosen translation methodology, I encountered a difficulty familiar to every translator: which sense of a word should I choose to convey the author's intended meaning as accurately as possible and avoid distortion?

The difficulty is that many Sanskrit concepts simply have no exact equivalents in Russian or English. The closest words convey only certain aspects of their meaning, while some concepts may be entirely absent from our familiar conceptual framework. By choosing a familiar word, we inadvertently confine meaning within the boundaries of our existing understanding.

I realized that if I settled on one of the possible interpretations of a Sanskrit term, it would most likely reflect my state of consciousness at that particular point in time, and that choice could be partly shaped by my conscious and subconscious assumptions and beliefs.

Yet later, as I deepen my practice and reach different states of consciousness, I may come to prefer different translations of the same terms, and those differences could be substantial. If I choose one particular meaning of a Sanskrit concept too early, I risk losing some of the depth the text has to offer.

By retaining the Sanskrit term in the text and providing its possible meanings in the accompanying notes, I can periodically relate my own experience to the text, discover new ways of understanding it, and deepen my practice.

Moreover, preserving Sanskrit concepts allows me to gradually expand my own conceptual framework rather than insisting on reducing the unknown to something already familiar. Perhaps these very concepts will prove closer to direct experience, for which our familiar modern languages do not yet have adequate words.

But why is understanding the precise meaning of this text so important to me?

The reason is that, for me, the *Pātañjalayogaśāstra* is not so much a philosophical theory as a genuinely practical teaching. Despite the absence of instructions as detailed as those found in some later traditions, such as Kriya Yoga in the lineage of Lahiri Mahasaya, and despite its many concepts that at first seem transcendent and rather abstract, the primary purpose of the text is to show a person the path toward attaining and directly experiencing certain states.

Interestingly, I first encountered this teaching many years ago. Back then, I understood it quite differently. Years later, through my own practice, I was surprised to discover that what had once seemed abstract had become entirely concrete. I began to find in the text answers to questions that arose, ways of overcoming difficulties, and direct guidance for action. And I can see how, as my practice deepens, my reading of the text changes and new horizons of the unknown open up.

For me, then, preserving Sanskrit terms means **giving myself the opportunity to uncover new facets of reality and directly experience what cannot be conveyed in words.**
